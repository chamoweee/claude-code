The no-allium pantry

Cooking without onion and garlic isn't about leaving things out — it's about knowing what to put in. These twelve ingredients do the work, and most cost very little.

If you buy only one thing

Buy asafoetida. A single small tub costs a few pounds, lasts a year, and is the difference between food that tastes like it's missing something and food that tastes complete. Everything else on this page is an improvement; hing is the foundation.

🌾

Asafoetida Essential

Hing, devil's dung

ReplacesGarlic

A dried resin from a giant fennel relative. Raw, it smells genuinely unpleasant — sulphurous and sharp. Bloomed in hot fat for a few seconds it transforms completely into something savory, rounded, and remarkably close to cooked garlic and onion. This single ingredient is why no-allium Indian cooking works at all.

How to use it

Add a pinch to hot oil or ghee at the very start of cooking, right after the whole spices. It needs fat and heat to bloom, and it only takes 10–15 seconds. Never add it to a cold pan or dry to a finished dish.

Buying & storing

Sold as a fine yellow powder in small plastic tubs, or as a hard lump you grate yourself. Most powdered brands are cut with rice or wheat flour — check the label if you eat gluten-free or keep a strict Jain diet. Store it airtight, away from other spices; it perfumes everything nearby.

Watch out

Use less than you think. A quarter teaspoon serves four people. Too much turns a dish bitter and medicinal, and it scorches in seconds on high heat.

Fresh ginger Essential

Adrak

ReplacesGarlic's bite, onion's warmth

The workhorse aromatic of an allium-free kitchen. Ginger brings pungency, warmth, and a clean heat that fills the space raw garlic normally occupies — without any of garlic's lingering harshness.

How to use it

Grate it on a microplane for gravies where you want it to disappear; julienne it when you want visible bite. Add it to the pan after the whole spices and cook 30 seconds before anything wet goes in. A little raw grated ginger stirred in at the end lifts a finished curry noticeably.

Buying & storing

Choose firm pieces with taut, thin skin — wrinkled ginger is dry and fibrous. Young ginger with pale pink tips is milder and juicier. It freezes well whole and grates easily straight from frozen.

Watch out

Scrape the skin off with the edge of a spoon rather than peeling with a knife; you'll waste far less.

Green chilies Essential

Hari mirch

ReplacesRaw garlic's sharpness

Fresh heat with a grassy, bright edge. Paired with ginger, green chili covers most of what raw garlic contributes to a dish — the sharp top note that makes food taste alive rather than flat.

How to use it

Slit them lengthwise and leave whole for gentle warmth that you can lift out later; chop them fine when you want real heat throughout. Adding them to the tempering mellows them; adding them raw at the end keeps them fierce.

Buying & storing

Indian green chilies are hotter and thinner than jalapeños. Serrano is the closest common substitute. They freeze perfectly — buy a lot and freeze them whole in a bag.

Watch out

Most of the heat is in the seeds and the pale membrane, not the flesh. Scrape them out to keep the flavor and lose the burn.

Curry leaves Essential

Kadi patta, meetha neem

ReplacesOnion's aromatic base

Nothing else tastes like this — faintly citrus, faintly nutty, deeply savory. In South Indian cooking a handful of curry leaves crackling in hot oil builds the aromatic foundation that a sautéed onion would elsewhere.

How to use it

Drop them into shimmering oil and stand back — they spit violently for about five seconds, then go crisp and release their scent. That's the tempering (tadka), and it's poured over the finished dish or used to start it.

Buying & storing

Fresh is the only version worth having; dried curry leaves taste like nothing. Buy them in bulk from an Indian grocer and freeze the whole sprigs — they keep for months and temper straight from frozen.

Watch out

They are not bay leaves and they are not curry powder. Entirely different plants, entirely different flavors.

Cumin seeds Essential

Jeera

ReplacesOnion's savory depth

Warm, earthy, and slightly bitter, cumin is the first thing in the pan in a huge share of these recipes. Toasted whole in fat, it gives the base note that slow-cooked onion provides in allium cooking.

How to use it

Always start with whole seeds in hot fat and wait for them to sizzle and darken by a shade — about 15 seconds. Ground cumin is a different ingredient with a different job; it goes in later, with the wet ingredients.

Buying & storing

Buy whole seeds and grind small batches yourself. Pre-ground cumin loses most of its aroma within a couple of months.

Watch out

Cumin goes from toasted to burnt fast. If it turns black and smells acrid, start over — the bitterness will run through the whole dish.

Black mustard seeds Essential

Rai, kadugu

ReplacesOnion's aromatic base

Tiny and sharp, mustard seeds pop in hot oil and turn nutty and mild. They open nearly every South and West Indian tempering, and their gentle pungency does some of the work that alliums would.

How to use it

Heat oil until it shimmers, add the seeds, and cover partially — they jump. Wait until the popping mostly stops before adding anything else, or the remaining seeds stay raw and bitter.

Buying & storing

The small black or brown variety, not yellow mustard seeds, which are milder and more European in character.

Watch out

If they aren't popping, the oil isn't hot enough — and unpopped mustard seeds taste harsh.

Amchur

Dried mango powder

ReplacesThe tang onion brings when caramelized

Unripe green mango, dried and ground to a pale, fibrous powder. It delivers a fruity sourness that brightens heavy dishes — the job a squeeze of something acidic does, without adding liquid.

How to use it

Stir it in near the end of cooking; prolonged heat dulls it. Classic in chana masala, samosa filling, and chaat.

Buying & storing

Any Indian grocer carries it cheaply. It clumps with humidity — keep it dry and break up lumps before measuring.

Watch out

Lemon juice substitutes at roughly one tablespoon per teaspoon of amchur, but it thins the dish, so reduce other liquid slightly.

Tamarind

Imli, puli

ReplacesDepth and sourness

A sticky, dark, sweet-sour pod pulp. Tamarind gives sambar, rasam, and many chutneys their backbone — a complex sourness with real depth to it, quite unlike vinegar or lemon.

How to use it

Concentrate from a jar is fine and fast: start with a teaspoon and taste. If using block tamarind, soak a walnut-sized piece in hot water for 15 minutes, squeeze it out, and strain off the fibres and seeds.

Buying & storing

Blocks are cheapest and taste best; jarred concentrate is far more convenient. Both keep almost indefinitely.

Watch out

Concentrates vary enormously in strength between brands. Always add gradually and taste.

Fresh coconut

Nariyal, thengai

ReplacesBody and richness from onion paste

Grated fresh coconut thickens chutneys and gravies and adds a sweet richness that keeps a dish from tasting thin — one of the main ways South Indian cooking builds body without an onion base.

How to use it

Grind it with green chili and cumin for chutney, or stir it into vegetable stir-fries at the end. Coconut oil, separately, is the right fat for Keralan dishes and genuinely changes the result.

Buying & storing

Frozen grated coconut is an excellent shortcut and far better than desiccated, which is dry and sweetened. Desiccated works only as a last resort, rehydrated in warm water.

Watch out

Fresh coconut sours within a day or two. Grate what you need and freeze the rest immediately.

Chickpea flour

Besan, gram flour

ReplacesBinding and body

Finely ground brown chickpeas. It binds koftas, batters pakoras, thickens gravies, and turns into pancakes — a genuinely versatile staple, and naturally gluten-free.

How to use it

Always toast or cook it properly; raw besan tastes chalky and unpleasant. In batters, rest the mixture ten minutes so the flour hydrates fully.

Buying & storing

Coarse besan is better for sweets like ladoo; fine besan suits batters. Both are cheap and keep a few months.

Watch out

It absorbs moisture from the air and clumps. Sift it if it's been open a while.

Ghee

Clarified butter

ReplacesRichness and mouthfeel

Butter with the milk solids cooked out and strained off, leaving pure, nutty fat with a very high smoke point. It's the traditional fat for blooming spices, and it carries aromas better than neutral oil.

How to use it

Use it wherever a recipe wants spices bloomed — it tolerates the heat asafoetida and cumin need without burning. A spoonful stirred into finished dal or rice at the table is standard practice and worth doing.

Buying & storing

Shelf-stable and long-lasting; no refrigeration required. Easy to make at home by simmering unsalted butter until the solids turn golden and smell nutty, then straining.

Watch out

Not vegan and not dairy-free. Coconut or a neutral oil substitutes fine in most savory dishes.

Carom seeds

Ajwain, bishop's weed

ReplacesSavory sharpness

Tiny seeds tasting strongly of thyme with a peppery bite. A small amount gives breads and batters a distinctive savory sharpness, and it's traditionally regarded as an aid to digestion — which is partly why it turns up in fried snacks.

How to use it

Crush lightly between your palms as you add it to release the oils. Standard in thepla, paratha, and besan batters.

Buying & storing

Widely available and inexpensive. A small packet lasts a very long time.

Watch out

Extremely potent. A half teaspoon flavors an entire batch of dough; more tastes medicinal.
